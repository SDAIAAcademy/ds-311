What a label is

A label is a measurement, with a construct and an error

Construct

What the label is meant to capture: "what the merchant refused", not "what went wrong".

Instrument

A person reading text under a guideline. Change the guideline and you change the instrument.

Error

Two people reading the same complaint give different labels. That spread is measurable.

So. A complaint rate by category is only as reliable as the labels under it. The analyst who publishes it owns that reliability.

Draw the parallel with a thermometer: a construct (temperature), an instrument, and an error you can estimate by measuring twice. Labels work the same way, and almost nobody measures them twice. That is what Day 1 does.

What to do with a split

Disagreement shows where the construct is unclear

  • Random disagreement: tiredness, a misread word. It spreads over every pair of categories.
  • Systematic disagreement: two readings of the schema. It piles up on one pair, such as DEFECT and REFUND.
  • Systematic splits are fixed by the guideline; random ones by training, workload and checks.
  • Some items are genuinely ambiguous: record them as edge cases rather than forcing a vote.

This distinction organises the whole day. Session 2 attacks systematic splits with a guideline, Session 3 measures how much is left, Session 4 adjudicates and revises. Day 2 handles the random kind with gold questions and workload limits.

What a labelling guideline holds

A guideline is the instrument, written down

Definitions

What each label measures, with includes and excludes.

Decision rules

An order that resolves every overlap to one label.

Edge cases

Decided examples raters can match against.

Language notes

Dialect words and code-switching that signal a fact.

Wellbeing

Who labels distressing content, and for how long.

Version and change log

Every label records the version it was made under.

Lab 1.2 writes all six parts as data, renders them to Markdown and lints them. Ask the room which part their own organisation's coding instructions usually lack: it is almost always the decision rules and the change log.

Excludesa refund asked and refused: REFUND; a warranty repair refused: WARRANTY
What goes wrong. "Excludes: other issues" leaves the rater to guess. The lab's lint fails an exclude that does not name another code.

The definition itself carries the decision: "and no refund or warranty repair was refused". Show how the excludes make the definition testable. Lint rules in the lab: at least eight words of definition, one include, one exclude that names another category.

Resolving overlaps

The first rule that applies decides

  1. A repair refused under warranty is WARRANTY, whatever else the complaint says.
  2. A refund asked for and refused is REFUND, even when the product was faulty or never arrived.
  3. A faulty, damaged or not-as-described product is DEFECT.
  4. A late or missing delivery is DELIVERY.
  5. A charge above the displayed price is PRICE, even when an advert was involved.
  6. An advert or offer not honoured, at the displayed price, is AD.

This order states the construct: the label measures the merchant's act the consumer is complaining about, from the most specific remedy (warranty) to the most general claim (advert). Another office could choose another order; what matters is that there is one and that it is written down.

Responsible labelling

The guideline says who labels distressing content, and for how long

  • About 3% of complaints describe threats or abuse by a merchant's staff.
  • Only raters who opted in and were briefed label them.
  • A daily limit, 60 minutes at Bayyin, and a break after every 20.
  • A risk to someone's safety goes to the duty officer the same day.

Why in the guideline

A limit nobody wrote down cannot be enforced or checked. On Day 2 you check it from the workload log.

The 60-minute limit is Bayyin's choice in this case, not a standard; say so. The catalog entry asks that the guideline record who may label distressing content and for how long. Lab 2.3 finds a rater who went past it on one day.

Chance agreement for two raters is the sum over categories of pa(c) x pb(c): how often they would match if each labelled at random with their own frequencies.

Numbers from the solution run of Lab 1.3 on the 300 pilot complaints, round 1. With six categories of similar size, chance is low (0.20). With one dominant category it can be 0.8 or more, and a high percent agreement means almost nothing. That is the next slide.

Why chance correction matters

With a dominant category, 90% agreement can be worth nothing

Rater A says "yes" 90%Rater B says "yes" 90%
Agreement by chance0.9 x 0.9 + 0.1 x 0.1 = 0.82
Observed agreement0.90
Kappa(0.90 - 0.82) / (1 - 0.82) = 0.44
What goes wrong. A rare category such as WARRANTY looks reliable on percent agreement because both raters almost always say "no".

Worked arithmetic, not a lab number. The opposite effect also exists (the kappa paradoxes: high agreement with low kappa when prevalence is skewed); Artstein and Poesio's survey discusses both. The fix is to report the chance-corrected figure and the prevalence together.

Sources: Artstein and Poesio, Inter-Coder Agreement for Computational Linguistics (2008).

Any number of raters, missing labels allowed

Krippendorff's alpha compares observed with expected disagreement

  1. Each complaint with m labels adds 1/(m-1) to the cell of every ordered pair of its labels.
  2. Do: the off-diagonal share of that coincidence matrix.
  3. De: the disagreement expected from the label totals alone.
  4. alpha = 1 - Do / De
0.594
alpha, round 1, three raters, 300 complaints

A rater who skipped twenty complaints changes it only to 0.591: alpha uses whatever labels each item has.

Walk through the four steps on the matrix from the lab. Alpha for nominal data is close to Fleiss' kappa for complete data, and it handles missing labels and other scales (ordinal, interval) with a different distance. The stretch of Lab 1.3 shows the missing-label case.

Sources: Krippendorff, Computing Krippendorff's Alpha-Reliability.

What the agreement report says

Report the number, its interval, and where it is weak

  • Overall alpha with a bootstrap interval over items.
  • Alpha by category, with the number of labels each rests on.
  • Alpha by rater pair or with each rater left out, to find one reader who differs.
  • Alpha by language variety.
  • The guideline version, the raters and the sample it was measured on.

There is no universal threshold. Krippendorff suggests relying on data with alpha of 0.8 or more and drawing tentative conclusions from 0.667 upwards; Bayyin's labelling lead sets her own threshold per use, which is the defensible practice. Avoid quoting a threshold without its source.

Sources: Krippendorff, Computing Krippendorff's Alpha-Reliability.

Checkpoint

Which agreement figure do you trust?

Two raters label whether a complaint is WARRANTY. They agree on 96% of 300 complaints; each says WARRANTY about 3% of the time. What is the best next step?

Accept: 96% agreement is excellent
Compute a chance-corrected figure and count the WARRANTY labels it rests on
Add a third rater and recompute percent agreement
Merge WARRANTY into DEFECT to raise agreement
Why. When both say "no" 97% of the time, chance agreement is about 94%; only a chance-corrected figure on enough WARRANTY labels shows whether they agree on the rare cases.

Answer: B. Chance agreement here is 0.97 x 0.97 + 0.03 x 0.03, about 0.94. Option D is tempting and sometimes right, but it is a decision about the construct, made with Hatoon who uses the WARRANTY figure, not a way to improve a statistic.

Common errors: computing De from the matrix diagonal instead of the row totals; forgetting the 1/(m-1) weight. The reference functions in bayyin.py let participants check their own. Quick mode uses 200 bootstrap resamples instead of 1,000; intervals move in the second decimal.

Three labels, one decision

A majority of three can be wrong in the same way

8 of 28
majorities in the 30 disagreements that the adjudicator overturned
  • Two raters who share a misreading outvote the one who read the rule.
  • A vote has no reason attached, so it teaches the guideline nothing.
  • Adjudication applies the guideline, and records why.

From the stretch of Lab 1.4: 28 of the 30 disagreements had a two-against-one majority, and 8 of those majorities were not the adjudicated label. Majority vote is fine for random noise; it fails for systematic misreadings, which is exactly what a pilot surfaces.
